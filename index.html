Speedup over tuned cache-only baselines · Qwen2.5-0.5B prefill on eight cores in gem5 · 1,024 tokens (FlashAttention 1,024–4,096)

The problemCacheFlex lets an ARM SVE core turn some ways of its private L2 into a software-managed scratchpad, reusing the cache arrays instead of adding a separate SRAM. On one core that is straightforward. On many cores, data copied into one core’s scratchpad sits outside the coherence domain while its source stays coherent, and the protocol has to keep the two consistent without flooding the network with invalidations.

What I didI designed and implemented the multicore coherence extension in gem5 Ruby/SLICC, wrote Rumur models to check it, and optimized the LLM prefill kernels that use it. Every core can snapshot the same read-shared weight panel into its own scratchpad while the original owner keeps its copy.

Protocol details
  • GETS_SILENT, a non-tracking read: the home supplies data but never adds the requester as a sharer. A remote owner answers with data and keeps its own state.
  • The source line keeps whatever coherence state it held before the copy. Copying into the scratchpad never releases or downgrades it.
  • Lazy migration for the way being claimed: a coherent line already in it moves to an open coherent way in the same set. If none is free, LRU decides which line to evict, which may be the occupant itself.
  • Transient states for silent fetch (IX_D) and scratchpad writeback.
  • Way-masked victim selection, so reserved ways are never evicted.
Verification and evaluation method

Rumur models check deadlock freedom, data ownership and legal transitions under concurrent requests. Kernel outputs are numerically validated, cache baselines are tuned by bounded search, and speedups include the cost of packing and staging data into the scratchpad.

Azilla, a chiplet Ising accelerator

Undergraduate researcher with Prof. Richard Shi. I designed the architecture and wrote its SystemVerilog datapaths and exact-event performance model.

1.65–1.74×
Toroidal graphs, to 1M

Modeled speedup in iteration cycles over a MAC-matched flat weight-stationary baseline

An Ising machine repeatedly multiplies a coupling matrix J by a vector of ±1 spins. At a million spins J is far too large for on-chip SRAM, so it streams from DRAM every iteration and memory bandwidth sets the speed.

Azilla puts the multiply engines at each chiplet’s DRAM interface. Because J is symmetric, one 32×32 int8 block read yields both Jabxb and JabTxa. Each block is fetched once per iteration, and the engine ships the two partial sums to the endpoint cores that own those spins.

What I built

  • SystemVerilog symmetric MVM engines (add/subtract trees on 256-bit rows), an 8-bank interleaved state SRAM with fixed-order arbitration, DRAM weight streamer, and partial-sum delivery into a FlooNoC mesh.
  • A C++ exact-event model that drives Ramulator2 HBM4 and GDDR6 timing and models network contention cycle by cycle. I verified it cycle-for-cycle against the RTL in VCS on 24 differential cases, up to a 65,536-spin dense problem.
  • A MAC-matched flat weight-stationary baseline, so the speedup measures the organization rather than extra compute.

Where it stops scaling

Model counters show the remaining limits. Dense runs are bound by the remote partial-sum port. Unstructured sparse graphs past 512K spins serialize on flush. The next redesigns are a wider port and early flush.

Post-layout

I compared master-slave flip-flops, a latch design with shared master latches, and an SRAM array, and chose shared master latches feeding per-register slave latches: fewer transistors and less clock load than flip-flops, and no precharge or sense amplifiers. At 13 entries, SRAM peripheral overhead would have outweighed its smaller cell.

  • A 4-to-13 decoder drives write enables onto a shared 16-bit data bus instead of per-bit demultiplexers.
  • An NMOS pull-down on each decoder output keeps enables from floating while the write address changes.
  • Minimum 50 nm channel length throughout, with wider devices only on storage feedback and the loaded read path.

Read energy is far above write energy because both read ports and their output loads dominate the switched capacitance.

How much do SWAPs cost a teleported qubit?

Controlled experiments on IBM’s ibm_fez with Tom Steen and Gary Zhou, in Qiskit.

SWAP distance
2–12 hops
Fidelity, unmitigated
≈0.75–0.88
Fidelity, mitigated
0.91–0.97

Teleportation fidelity on ibm_fez, runtime-padded circuits, mean of three runs

Teleportation fidelity drops as the EPR pair is moved further apart. A longer SWAP chain adds gate error and also makes the circuit run longer, so idle decoherence grows at the same time. To separate the two, we padded every circuit with delays to the duration of the 12-hop case.

Fidelity fell with distance in both padded and unpadded runs, and padded runs were generally lower. The gap was smaller than expected and the error bars overlap at 6 hops, which points to the SWAPs themselves as the dominant loss.

Error mitigation

We then ran the padded set with Pauli twirling, zero-noise extrapolation and twirled readout-error mitigation. Mean fidelity rose to 0.91–0.97, with much larger variance, and a few raw estimates exceeded 1. These are extrapolated estimates, not a physical reduction in error, and backend calibration drift across runs adds to the spread.
